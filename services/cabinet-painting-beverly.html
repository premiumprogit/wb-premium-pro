Painting vs. Refinishing vs. Refacing vs. Replacing

These four are very different projects with very different budgets. Here is the honest breakdown for your Beverly kitchen:

  • Cabinet painting keeps your existing boxes, doors and fronts and gives them a new sprayed finish and color. Best when the boxes are solid, and the most affordable way to remake a kitchen.
  • Refinishing strips to the wood and re-stains or clear-coats to show the grain, best when you want a natural wood look.
  • Refacing keeps the boxes but replaces the doors and visible surfaces with new material. More than painting, less than replacing.
  • Replacing tears everything out for new cabinets. The most expensive and disruptive, worth it only when the boxes are damaged or the layout does not work.

We will tell you honestly which one fits. For most Beverly kitchens with sound boxes, painting is the smart money, and when replacement genuinely makes more sense, we will say so.

How We Spray a Factory-Smooth Cabinet Finish

Professional-looking cabinets come from the process, not just the can of paint. Here is how we do it: 1. Remove and label. Every door and drawer front comes off and is labeled to return to its exact spot. 2. Clean and degrease. Cooking grease destroys adhesion, so we degrease every surface thoroughly before anything else. 3. Sand and prep. We sand all surfaces so the primer can grip, fill dents and grain as needed, and mask the kitchen to control dust and overspray. 4. Bonding primer. We prime with a high-adhesion bonding primer made for slick, previously finished surfaces, the biggest single factor in whether a finish lasts or peels. 5. Spray the finish. Doors and fronts are sprayed in a controlled setup and boxes in place, for a smooth, even, hard topcoat with no brush marks. 6. Cure and reassemble. We let the finish cure fully, then rehang doors, reinstall drawers and hardware, and adjust everything to close cleanly.

Why Prep Is the Whole Game

Cabinet finishes fail almost always for one reason: they were painted over grease and gloss without real prep. Cabinet surfaces are slick and coated in kitchen residue, so paint that is not degreased, sanded and properly bonded will chip and peel on the high-touch edges within months. Our degreasing, sanding and bonding primer are not extras, they are the whole reason a finish turns durable and factory-like instead of failing fast. It is the least glamorous part of the job and by far the most important, and no premium topcoat can make up for prep that was skipped.

Spray Finish vs. Brush and Roll: Why Sprayed Cabinets Last

A brushed or rolled cabinet door looks painted; a sprayed one looks like factory-finished millwork, and in a Beverly kitchen that difference shows every day. A brush leaves marks and a roller leaves stipple, both of which the light picks up on flat panels and top rails. Spraying atomizes the coating into a fine mist that self-levels into a smooth, even surface with no texture or lap lines, and it lets us lay down a harder, cabinet-grade coating that is hard to brush well. That is the whole reason professionals spray, and it is why a sprayed finish holds up for years while a DIY brush job chips on the edges within months of daily use.

Cabinet Materials in Beverly Kitchens: What Can Be Painted

Beverly's mix of older homes and newer construction means every kind of cabinet material turns up, and each takes a different approach. Solid wood, common in the city's established neighborhoods, is the ideal candidate and paints beautifully once degreased, sanded and primed, with a grain filler available for a perfectly smooth result on open-grain species. MDF shaker doors have no grain and finish flawlessly. Thermofoil requires a specialized bonding primer and any peeling foil has to be fixed first. Particle board or laminate boxes with intact surfaces can be painted with the right bonding primer, while swollen or crumbling board is past saving. We check your specific cabinets at the estimate and tell you honestly whether painting will give you a lasting result.

A Beverly Cabinet Project, Day by Day

A typical Beverly cabinet painting project runs about four to seven working days, and that timeline is the prep and cure that make the finish last. Day one we remove and label every door and drawer front, pull hardware, and mask and protect the kitchen. Day two is thorough degreasing and sanding, filling dents and grain. Day three is the bonding primer on boxes in place and doors in a controlled setup. Days four and five are the sprayed finish coats with correct flash time between them. Day six is cure and reassembly, rehanging doors, reinstalling drawers and hardware, and fine-tuning everything to close cleanly. The finish continues hardening to full durability for a couple of weeks after we leave.

Prep for Greasy and Heavily Used Kitchens

A busy Beverly family kitchen carries years of cooking grease and residue, and painting over it is the leading cause of a cabinet finish failing. Our prep is designed to prevent exactly that. We degrease every surface thoroughly, repeating where buildup is heavy, then sand and apply a bonding primer so the finish grips clean substrate instead of oily film. The areas around the range, sink and trash pull get extra attention because they see the most grease and the most wear.
