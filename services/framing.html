What a Framing Contractor Actually Does on the North Shore

A framing contractor builds the structural skeleton of a house: the floor system, the walls, the headers and beams, and the roof. It is the least glamorous stage of any project and the most important, because every finished surface you will ever see, drywall, trim, tile, cabinets, hangs on the frame underneath it. When the frame is plumb, level and square, everything built on top of it sits true and stays true. When the frame is off, you get doors that will not close, cracks that keep coming back, and floors that dip, and no amount of good finish work hides it. That is why we treat framing as the foundation of quality, not a rough stage to rush through.

On the North Shore we frame two very different kinds of jobs, and both take a crew that knows the difference. New construction and additions are about building a clean, code-correct structure from scratch: laying out the plate lines, cutting and setting the floor, wall and roof framing, and getting to a weathertight, inspected shell. Structural repair and remodeling is the opposite problem, working inside a home that is already there, where a wall you want to remove is holding up the second floor, a floor bounces because a joist was overspanned decades ago, or a sill has quietly rotted behind the siding. We do both, and because we are a full general contractor rather than a framing sub who disappears after the shell, we carry the job through the inspection and into the finish.

Rough Framing vs Finish Carpentry, and Why the Difference Matters

People often lump all carpentry together, but rough framing and finish carpentry are two different trades with two different goals. Rough framing is structural: it is the floor system, the wall studs and plates, the headers and beams, and the roof framing that create the shape and carry the loads of the house. It is measured in whether it is plumb, level, square and strong enough, and it is the stage the building department checks at the rough-frame inspection. Finish carpentry is what you see: baseboard, casing, crown, doors, built-ins and detail. It is measured in whether the miters are tight and the lines are clean.

The reason the difference matters to you is that finish work can only be as good as the frame under it. A trim carpenter can be a genuine artist and still fight a wall that is out of plumb or a floor that runs downhill, and the compromises show up as gaps, shims and reveals that never look right. When the same accountable crew handles the structural carpentry and understands what the finish stage needs, the frame gets built with the finish in mind: openings sized correctly, backing added where cabinets and railings will land, and walls dead plumb so the trim later goes on clean. That coordination is exactly what a homeowner loses when framing gets handed to a low-bid sub and the finish crew inherits their mistakes.

Stick-Built vs Engineered Lumber: LVL, LSL and I-Joists

Not all framing lumber is the same, and choosing the right material for each part of the structure is part of framing it correctly. Traditional stick-built framing uses dimensional lumber, the familiar two-by material, and it is still the right choice for most wall framing and plenty of floor and roof work. But modern framing also leans heavily on engineered wood products where a longer span or a heavier load makes solid lumber impractical, and knowing when to use which is the difference between a frame that performs and one that bounces or sags.

Engineered lumber includes LVL (laminated veneer lumber) for strong, straight beams and headers, LSL (laminated strand lumber) for tall studs and rim board, and engineered I-joists for long, stiff floor spans that resist the bounce older solid-joist floors are known for. Where a span or a point load is really demanding, a steel beam is the right call. We spec these products from the engineer's numbers, not by guesswork, so a load-bearing wall removal or a wide-open floor plan is carried by a beam that is actually sized for the job. Using engineered lumber correctly is one of the quiet reasons a modern frame stays flat and quiet for decades.

The Framing Process, Step by Step

A framing job runs in a clear sequence, and understanding it helps you see where the value is. It starts with the plans and, where a beam or a removal is involved, an engineer's stamped sizing, because framing correctly begins long before the first stud is cut. From there we pull the permit and lay out the plate lines precisely off the foundation or the existing structure, checking for level and square before anything goes vertical. On new work the floor system goes down first, then the walls are framed, stood and braced plumb, then the beams and headers are set, and finally the roof is framed or the trusses are set and braced.

Once the shell is up, sheathing goes on the walls and roof, the rough openings for windows and doors are set true, and the structure is tied together and braced so it acts as one rigid unit. Then we call the rough-frame inspection, and only after it is signed off does the job move on to the mechanical rough-in, insulation and drywall. On a structural repair the sequence is different, temporarily shoring the loads, opening up carefully, replacing or reinforcing the framing, and then rebuilding, but the principle is the same: get the structure right and inspected before anything closes it up. That discipline is what separates a frame you never think about again from one that comes back to haunt the finished rooms.

Blueprints, Engineering and Inspections

Good framing is a paperwork trade as much as a hammer trade. Before we frame an addition, a beam or a second story, we work from the drawings and, when the loads require it, from a structural engineer's stamped design that specifies beam sizes, connections, post locations and footing sizes. We would rather pay for engineering and build a beam that is genuinely sized for the span than eyeball it and hope, because a load-bearing element that is undersized does not fail loudly on day one, it sags slowly and cracks everything above it. As your general contractor we coordinate that engineering so you are not chasing it yourself.

The other half is the building department. Framing that touches structure needs a permit, and the frame has to pass a rough-frame inspection before it can be covered. We pull the permit, build to the approved plans, and schedule the inspection, and we do not let drywall or insulation close in a frame that has not been signed off. That inspection is not an obstacle, it is protection: it confirms your frame is to code and it becomes part of the record that protects your home's value at resale. Skipping it to save a few days is exactly the kind of shortcut that turns into a real problem when you eventually sell.

Framing to Massachusetts Building Code

Every frame we build is nailed and connected to the Massachusetts State Building Code, which in New England means real attention to snow and wind loads, proper connections and bracing, and correctly sized structural members. It is not enough for a frame to look sturdy, it has to meet the span tables, the nailing schedules and the connection requirements that the code and the inspector are checking against. A crew that frames by habit rather than by the code can build something that stands but does not pass, and then the fix happens after the fact, which is slow and expensive.

Because we frame to code from the start and know what the local inspectors on the North Shore look for, our rough-frame inspections tend to go smoothly and on the first visit. That is not luck, it is building the frame right the first time: hangers and hardware where they belong, headers and beams sized to the plan, bracing and blocking in place, and the load path carried cleanly from the roof down to the foundation. Getting the frame signed off cleanly keeps the whole project on schedule, because nothing downstream can start until the framing inspection passes.

How Framing Quality Affects Everything Built on Top of It

Homeowners rarely see the frame, so it is easy to underrate it, but the frame quietly decides how the finished house feels for as long as you own it. Drywall hangs flat and stays crack-free when the studs behind it are plumb and in plane. Trim and doors fit cleanly and keep working when the openings are square and the walls are true. Cabinets and countertops sit level and the doors line up when the floor and the walls they mount to are straight. Tile stays uncracked when the floor beneath it is stiff and flat. Every one of those finished results is really a framing result wearing a nicer surface.

The reverse is just as true and much more expensive. A frame that is out of plumb, a floor that bounces or a beam that sags shows up months or years later as cracked drywall, sticking doors, gaps at the trim, and countertops that no longer sit flush, and by then the frame is buried behind everything. Fixing a framing mistake after the house is finished can mean tearing out finished rooms to reach it. That is why paying attention at the framing stage is the cheapest quality insurance in the whole project, and why we frame as if the finish depends on it, because it does.

Structural Issues in Older North Shore Homes

The North Shore has some of the oldest housing stock in the country, and that age is exactly why these homes have character and exactly why they develop structural issues. The most common thing we find is settled and bouncy floors, usually because the original joists were undersized by today's standards or were overspanned, sometimes made worse by a carrying beam that has sagged or by generations of plumbers and electricians notching and drilling the framing to run their lines. The fix is rarely mysterious once you find the real cause: sister the joists, add or resize a beam and its posts, and restore a proper load path.

The other recurring problem is rot from moisture, most often at the sill plate and band joist where the wood frame meets the foundation. Water from grading, gutters, a leaking deck ledger or old flashing gets in over the years and quietly turns the bottom of the frame soft, which is both a structural problem and an entry point for pests. We cut out and replace the rotted sill and framing, but just as importantly we correct the source of the water so the repair actually lasts. Older homes also hide framing that was cut into for a stairway or a chimney that is no longer supported the way it should be. Working on these homes takes a framing contractor who expects the surprises instead of being rattled by them, budgets for them honestly up front, and fixes the cause rather than the symptom.

Framing Elements We Build and Repair

A frame is made of many distinct structural parts, and each one has to be sized and connected correctly for the whole to work. Here are the elements our crews build and repair most often on the North Shore, and why each one matters to the finished home.

Floor Joists and Subfloor

Floor joists carry the live load of everything you put on a floor, and the subfloor ties them together into a stiff platform. When joists are undersized, overspanned or damaged, the floor feels bouncy and can crack tile and grout above. We frame new floor systems, add mid-span support, sister weak joists, and lay a properly glued and fastened subfloor so the floor is flat, quiet and solid.

Sill Plates and Band Joists

The sill plate is the piece of framing that sits on the foundation and everything above ties into it, and the band joist wraps the ends of the floor joists. Because they live where wood meets concrete and where water tends to find its way in, they are the most common spot for hidden rot in an older home. We replace rotted sills and band joists and correct the moisture source so the base of your frame is sound again.

Wall Studs, Plates and Sheathing

Wall framing is studs between top and bottom plates, sheathed on the outside to brace the wall and tie it together. Getting walls plumb and in plane is what lets drywall hang flat and trim fit clean later. We frame exterior and interior walls straight and true, add the blocking and backing that cabinets, railings and fixtures need, and sheathe and brace so the walls resist wind and racking.

Headers Over Windows and Doors

Every opening in a load-bearing wall needs a header to carry the weight above it across the gap. An undersized or missing header lets the wall sag over the opening, which shows up as doors and windows that stick. We size headers to the span and the load, from dimensional lumber to LVL, so openings stay square and operate smoothly for the life of the house.

Beams, Posts and Load Paths

When you remove a wall or span a wide opening, a beam takes over the load, and it only works if the posts under it carry that load in a continuous path down to a footing. This is the part amateurs get wrong: a beautiful beam that dead-ends onto a floor instead of a footing will sag and drop everything above. We build the whole load path, beam, posts and footings, from the engineer's numbers.

Rafters, Ridge and Roof Trusses

Roof framing has to carry New England snow and wind and hold a straight roofline for decades. We frame stick-built roofs with rafters and a structural ridge for custom shapes and dormers, and we set and brace engineered trusses where they are the right, faster solution. Either way the roof is tied to the walls with proper connections so it stays put in a storm.

Dormers and Roofline Changes

A dormer adds headroom and light to an attic or a story-and-a-half Cape, but it means cutting into an existing roof and reframing the opening so the structure still carries its loads. We frame shed and gable dormers tied cleanly into the existing roof, keeping the water shedding correctly and the new framing fully supported, so you gain usable space without a leak or a sag.

Shoring and Temporary Support

Before a load-bearing wall comes out or a beam is replaced, the loads above have to be picked up temporarily so nothing moves. Proper shoring is the difference between a clean structural job and cracked ceilings and dropped floors. We plan and build temporary support first, make the structural change, then transfer the load onto the permanent beam, all controlled so the finished rooms above never know it happened.

Framing for Decks and Porches

Deck and porch framing is structural work exposed to the weather, and it is where a lot of failures start: an under-bolted ledger, undersized joists, or posts that are not properly footed. We frame decks and porches with correct ledger flashing and attachment, proper joist and beam sizing, and footings below frost, so the structure is safe underfoot and lasts in New England conditions.
