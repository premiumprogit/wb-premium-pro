Painting vs. Refinishing vs. Refacing vs. Replacing

Homeowners often use these words interchangeably, but they are very different projects with very different costs. Here is the honest version so you can decide what actually fits your kitchen:

  • Cabinet painting keeps your existing boxes, doors and drawer fronts and gives them a new sprayed finish and color. Best when the boxes are solid and the layout works. The most affordable way to transform a kitchen.
  • Refinishing usually means stripping back to the wood and re-staining or clear-coating to show the grain. Best when you want a natural wood look rather than a painted color.
  • Refacing keeps the boxes but replaces the doors, drawer fronts and visible surfaces with new material. More expensive than painting, less than replacing.
  • Replacing tears out the old cabinets and installs all-new ones. The most expensive and most disruptive, and the right call only when the boxes are damaged, poorly laid out or beyond saving.

We will tell you honestly which one your kitchen calls for. If the boxes are sound, painting is almost always the smart money, and when they are not, we will say so.

How We Spray a Factory-Smooth Cabinet Finish

The difference between cabinets that look professionally done and cabinets that look painted is all in the process. Here is ours: 1. Remove and label. We take off every door and drawer front and label them so each goes back exactly where it belongs. 2. Clean and degrease. Kitchen cabinets carry years of cooking grease and grime that will wreck adhesion. We degrease every surface thoroughly. 3. Sand and prep. We sand all surfaces to give the primer something to grip, fill dents and grain where needed, and mask off the kitchen to control dust and overspray. 4. Bonding primer. We prime with a high-adhesion bonding primer made for slick, previously finished surfaces, which is the single biggest reason a cabinet finish either lasts or peels. 5. Spray the finish. Doors and fronts are sprayed in a controlled setup and boxes are sprayed in place, laying down a smooth, even, hard topcoat with no brush marks. 6. Cure and reassemble. We let the finish cure properly, then rehang doors, reinstall drawers and hardware, and adjust everything to close cleanly.

Why Prep Is the Whole Game With Cabinets

Cabinet finishes fail for one reason more than any other: they were painted over grease and gloss without proper prep. Cabinet surfaces are slick and coated in kitchen residue, so paint that is not properly degreased, sanded and bonded will chip, peel and show wear on the high-touch edges within months. Our process exists to prevent exactly that. The degreasing, sanding and bonding primer are not extras, they are what turn paint into a durable, factory-like finish that survives daily use, cleaning and the constant open-and-close of a working kitchen. Skimp on prep and no topcoat, however expensive, will save the job.

Done right, a sprayed cabinet finish holds up for many years of daily kitchen use. Longevity comes down to two things: prep and product. Spray Finish vs. Brush and Roll: Why Sprayed Cabinets Last

Brush and roll a cabinet door and it will look painted, with the ridges and stipple that Peabody kitchen light catches at every angle. Spray it and it looks like factory-finished millwork. Spraying atomizes the coating into a fine mist that self-levels into a glass-smooth surface with no brush marks, drips or lap lines, and it lets us apply a harder, cabinet-grade coating that is difficult to brush well but sprays beautifully. That smoothness on the flat panels and top rails, where the eye lands, is the whole reason professionals spray. It is also why a sprayed finish holds up for years while a brushed DIY coat chips on the edges within months.

Cabinet Materials: Can We Paint Solid Wood, MDF, Thermofoil and Particle Board?

Peabody kitchens run the full range of cabinet materials, and the material dictates the approach. Solid wood boxes and doors, common in the city's older colonials and ranches, are the ideal candidate and paint beautifully once degreased, sanded and primed; open-grain oak can take a grain filler for a perfectly smooth result. MDF, found on many modern shaker doors, has no grain and takes a flawless finish. Thermofoil, a vinyl film over MDF, is paintable but needs a specialized bonding primer, and any peeling foil near heat has to be addressed first. Particle board with an intact laminate surface can be painted with the right bonding primer, but swollen or crumbling board is past saving. We check your specific cabinets at the estimate and tell you honestly what will hold.

A Peabody Cabinet Project, Day by Day

Homeowners are often surprised cabinet painting takes most of a week, but that time is the prep and cure that make the finish last. A typical Peabody project runs about four to seven working days. Day one we remove and label every door and drawer front, pull hardware, and mask and protect the kitchen. Day two is deep degreasing and sanding, filling dents and grain. Day three is the bonding primer on boxes in place and doors in a controlled setup. Days four and five are the sprayed finish coats with proper flash time between them. Day six is cure and reassembly, rehanging doors, reinstalling drawers and hardware, and adjusting everything to close cleanly. The finish keeps hardening for a couple of weeks after we leave.

Prep for Greasy and Heavily Used Kitchens

A hard-working Peabody family kitchen carries years of cooking grease and residue that will wreck a cabinet finish if it is painted over, and it is the number one cause of peeling. This is where our prep earns its keep. We degrease every surface thoroughly, often more than once on heavily used cabinets, then sand and apply a bonding primer so the finish grips clean substrate instead of oily film. Areas around the range, the trash pull and the sink take extra attention because they see the most contamination and the most wear. Skip this and no topcoat will save the job, which is exactly why a real prep process, not a low bid, is what makes a kitchen finish last.
