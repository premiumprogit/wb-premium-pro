Cabinet Painting vs. Cabinet Refinishing vs. Refacing

Homeowners use these words interchangeably, but they describe different levels of work, and the right one depends on the condition of your cabinets and the look you are after. Knowing the difference before your estimate helps you plan the budget honestly.

Cabinet painting is the most common and highest-return option. We keep your existing doors, drawer fronts and boxes, prep them properly and spray a new finish in the color you choose. It is the fastest way to turn a dated oak or builder-grade kitchen into a clean, modern one, and for most North Shore kitchens with solid boxes it is the smartest money you can spend.

Cabinet refinishing is painting plus the carpentry that makes it last: repairing or replacing worn hinges, fixing split fronts, filling and sanding damage, and squaring anything that has drifted. As a general contractor this is where we shine, because the carpentry and the finish are handled by the same team on the same schedule instead of one trade waiting on another.

Refacing replaces the doors and drawer fronts and applies a new veneer to the visible box faces, while keeping the cabinet frames. It costs more than painting and less than full replacement, and it makes sense when the doors themselves are the problem but the boxes are sound. We will tell you honestly when refacing is worth it and when a spray finish gets you the same look for less.

Replacement is the full tear-out, and it is the right answer when the boxes are failing, the layout is wrong, or you are changing the footprint of the kitchen. That is a kitchen remodeling project, and it is one we do all the time, but we never push you toward it when your existing cabinets can be saved.

Colors and Finishes That Work in North Shore Kitchens

Color is where cabinet painting gets fun, and where a lot of DIY jobs go sideways. We color-match from any sample, brand chip or piece of your existing decor, so you are not limited to a small deck of options. That said, some choices simply photograph and age better than others in the kind of light most North Shore kitchens get.

Soft whites and warm off-whites remain the safest, most resale-friendly choice, brightening a kitchen without the cold hospital look that pure bright white can give under LED lighting. Greige and muted greens have become popular on lower cabinets and islands, paired with a lighter upper, which adds depth without committing the whole room to a bold color. Deep navy and charcoal islands read expensive and hide daily wear well, and near a coast they feel right at home. For the finish sheen, we generally recommend a satin or semi-gloss on cabinets, durable enough to wipe down and forgiving enough to hide minor imperfections, rather than a flat that shows every fingerprint.

We also think about how the cabinet color lands against your counters, backsplash, floor and wall paint, because cabinets never live in isolation. If we are refreshing the cabinets, it is often worth tying the wall and trim color into the same plan through interior painting, so the whole room reads as one intentional design instead of a repainted set of doors in an otherwise dated room.

Why Prep Decides Whether a Cabinet Finish Lasts

Almost every failed cabinet paint job traces back to prep, not paint. Cabinet doors near a stove and sink collect a thin film of cooking grease and hand oils that no finish will bond to. If a painter sprays over that film, the finish looks great for a few weeks and then starts to peel at the edges and around the handles where hands touch it most. That is the number one reason homeowners who tried a weekend DIY job end up calling a general contractor to redo it properly.

Our prep is deliberately slow, because slow is what makes it fast in the long run. Every door, drawer front and box face is degreased with the right cleaner, not just wiped down, then scuff-sanded so the primer has a mechanical grip as well as a chemical one. We fill dents, dings and old hardware holes when the layout is changing, sand those repairs flush, and dust everything off before priming. Only then does a bonding primer go on, followed by the sprayed finish coats. It is not glamorous work, and it is the part a homeowner never sees, which is exactly why it is the part that gets skipped by cut-rate crews and never skipped by us.

Living With Painted Cabinets: Care and Longevity

Homeowners often ask how a painted finish holds up to real kitchen life, and the honest answer is that it holds up very well when it was prepped and sprayed correctly, and it is easy to keep looking new. A properly cured cabinet-grade finish is washable, so day-to-day cleaning is just a soft cloth with mild soap and water, the same as you would treat any quality painted surface. We recommend giving the finish a couple of weeks to fully cure before scrubbing hard or hanging towels over freshly painted doors, and after that it is genuinely low maintenance.

Over many years of daily use, the spots that see the most contact, around the sink, the trash pull-out and the most-used drawers, are the first to show any wear, and because we spray rather than brush, any future touch-up blends cleanly instead of leaving a patch. That is another quiet advantage of hiring one accountable contractor: we keep a record of your color and finish, so if you ever want a touch-up or want to extend the same look to another part of the home, we can match it exactly rather than guessing.
