Cabinet Painting for Salem's Older Kitchens

Salem is a city of colonials, Victorians, sea-captain homes and dense downtown houses, many of them centuries old, and the kitchens inside them tell that story. A lot of Salem cabinet boxes are solid, well-built hardwood that has simply gone out of style, dark stained oak from a past remodel, honey maple, or a color that no longer suits a home the owner wants to modernize. In older homes especially, those boxes are often better made than a lot of what you can buy new today, and they are worth keeping. Painting them brings a period home's kitchen up to date while respecting the character of the house, and it costs a fraction of new cabinetry.

Painting vs. Refinishing vs. Refacing vs. Replacing

These terms get mixed up, but they are very different projects. Here is the straight version so you can choose what fits your Salem kitchen:

  • Cabinet painting keeps your existing boxes, doors and fronts and gives them a fresh sprayed finish and color. Best when the boxes are solid, and the most affordable transformation.
  • Refinishing strips back to the wood to re-stain or clear-coat, best when you want the natural grain to show, which appeals in some older Salem homes.
  • Refacing keeps the boxes but swaps the doors and visible surfaces for new material. Pricier than painting, cheaper than replacing.
  • Replacing removes everything and installs new cabinets. The most costly and disruptive, and only worth it when the boxes are damaged, poorly laid out or past saving.

We will tell you honestly which your kitchen needs. In older Salem homes with solid hardwood boxes, painting is usually the smart money, and when the boxes are shot, we will say so.

How We Spray a Factory-Smooth Cabinet Finish

The professional look comes entirely from the process, not just the paint. Here is ours: 1. Remove and label. Every door and drawer front comes off and is labeled to return to its exact spot. 2. Clean and degrease. Years of cooking grease will ruin adhesion, so we degrease every surface thoroughly first. 3. Sand and prep. We sand all surfaces so primer can grip, fill dents and open grain where needed, and mask the kitchen to control dust and overspray. 4. Bonding primer. We prime with a high-adhesion bonding primer built for slick, previously finished surfaces, the single biggest factor in whether a finish lasts or peels. 5. Spray the finish. Doors and fronts are sprayed in a controlled setup and boxes in place, for a smooth, even, hard topcoat with no brush marks. 6. Cure and reassemble. We let the finish cure properly, then rehang doors, reinstall drawers and hardware, and adjust everything to close cleanly.

Why Prep Decides Everything

Cabinet finishes fail for one reason above all: they were painted over grease and gloss without proper prep. Cabinet surfaces are slick and coated in kitchen residue, so any finish that is not degreased, sanded and properly bonded will chip and peel on the high-touch edges within months. This matters even more in older Salem homes where cabinets have accumulated decades of grime. Our degreasing, sanding and bonding primer are not add-ons, they are what turn paint into a durable, factory-like finish that survives a real working kitchen. No topcoat can rescue a job that skipped the prep.

How Long a Painted Cabinet Finish Lasts

A properly sprayed cabinet finish holds up for many years of daily use. Longevity comes down to prep and product: because we degrease, sand and bond-prime, the finish grips instead of sitting on grease, and because we spray a hard, durable topcoat rather than brushing on wall paint, it stands up to hands, cleaning and steam. Clean it with mild soap rather than harsh abrasives and wipe spills promptly, and a quality painted cabinet job should look great for a decade or more. When it is eventually time for a refresh, it is far simpler and cheaper than replacing the cabinets.

Spray Finish vs. Brush and Roll: Why Sprayed Cabinets Last

In a Salem kitchen, the difference between cabinets that look professionally done and cabinets that look repainted is entirely in the method. A brush leaves marks and a roller leaves texture, both of which the light in an older home catches at every angle. Spraying atomizes the coating into a fine mist that lays down flat and self-levels into a glass-smooth surface, and it lets us apply a harder, cabinet-grade coating built to survive daily use. On the flat panels of shaker and recessed-panel doors, that smoothness is what makes painted cabinets read like new millwork. It is also why a sprayed finish lasts for years while a brushed DIY attempt chips at the edges within a season or two.

Cabinet Materials in Older Salem Kitchens: What Can Be Painted

Salem's colonials, Victorians and downtown homes hold a wide range of cabinet materials, and the material decides the approach. Solid wood, common in older Salem kitchens and often better built than new stock, is the ideal candidate and takes paint beautifully once degreased, sanded and primed; open-grain species can take a grain filler for a perfectly smooth result. MDF doors have no grain and finish flawlessly. Thermofoil needs a specialized bonding primer and any peeling foil must be repaired first. Particle board with an intact surface can be painted with the correct bonding primer, but swollen or delaminating board is past saving. We inspect your specific cabinets at the estimate and tell you honestly what will hold, because painting is a finish, not a structural fix.

A Salem Cabinet Project, Day by Day

A typical Salem cabinet painting project runs about four to seven working days, and nearly all of that is the prep and cure that make the finish durable. Day one we remove and label the doors and drawer fronts, pull hardware, and mask and protect the kitchen. Day two is thorough degreasing and sanding, with older Salem kitchens often needing extra degreasing for decades of buildup. Day three is the bonding primer on boxes in place and doors in a controlled setup. Days four and five are the sprayed finish coats with proper flash time. Day six is cure and reassembly, rehanging doors, reinstalling drawers and hardware, and adjusting for a clean close. The finish keeps curing to full hardness for a couple of weeks afterward.

Prep for Greasy and Well-Used Older Kitchens

Older Salem kitchens have often cooked a lot of meals, and decades of grease and residue are the number one enemy of a cabinet finish. Paint over that film and it peels, no matter the topcoat. Our prep is built to prevent it: we degrease every surface thoroughly, repeating on heavily used cabinets, then sand and apply a bonding primer so the finish grips clean substrate. The zones around the range, sink and trash pull get extra attention because they carry the most contamination and see the most wear. This unglamorous step is the single biggest reason our Salem cabinet finishes hold up, and it is exactly what the cheapest bids skip.
