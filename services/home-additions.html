Home additions on the North Shore: what we build

A home addition is the biggest project most families ever take on, and on the North Shore it usually means adding space to a house that is decades or centuries old, on a lot that is tighter than anyone would like. We build the whole addition under one contract: engineering coordination, permits, foundation, framing, roofing, exterior, licensed plumbing, electrical and HVAC, insulation, drywall, trim and paint.

  • Family room and great room additions off the back of the house
  • Primary suite additions with a bathroom and closet
  • Second-story additions over an existing floor or garage
  • Kitchen bump-outs that make room for an island or a dining area
  • Mudrooms and entries that finally give boots and coats a home
  • Dormers that turn a half story into full bedrooms

Should you add on, or use the space you already have?

Before designing an addition, it is worth asking whether an unfinished basement, an attic, a garage or a better layout would solve the problem for less. Sometimes removing a wall does more than adding 200 square feet. Sometimes a dormer turns an attic into two bedrooms without touching the footprint.

When an addition is the right answer, it is usually because the family needs rooms on the main floor, a real primary suite, or space the existing house simply cannot give. We talk through those options honestly at the first walkthrough, before any money changes hands.

Bump-outs: small additions with a big payoff

A bump-out extends one wall of a room by a few feet. It is the most affordable kind of addition and one of the most effective in kitchens, where two or three extra feet can make space for an island or a proper eating area. Some bump-outs can be cantilevered off the existing floor framing, while others need their own foundation, depending on size and the engineer's review.

Family room and great room additions

The classic North Shore addition is a family room off the back of a Colonial or Cape, often with a vaulted ceiling, lots of windows and a direct connection to the kitchen. The design challenge is making it feel like part of the house, not a room tacked on. That means matching floor heights, carrying trim details through and planning how the roof meets the existing house.

Primary suite additions

Many older homes on the North Shore have small bedrooms and a single shared bath. A primary suite addition adds a bedroom, a full bathroom and closet space, often on the first floor for owners planning to stay in the house long term. The bathroom inside the addition gets the same waterproofing and tile standards as our bathroom remodeling work.

Second-story additions

Building up keeps your yard and avoids many lot-coverage limits, but it is a major structural project. The existing walls and foundation have to carry the new floor and roof, which means an engineer reviews the whole load path, and the family often needs to plan around the roof being opened. Framing is where these projects are won or lost, and our framing contractor page explains how we handle it.

Zoning, setbacks and lot coverage

Every North Shore town has a zoning bylaw that controls how close you can build to property lines, how much of the lot the house can cover and how tall it can be. Many older neighborhoods were built before those rules, so existing homes are often nonconforming, and an addition may need a special permit or variance from the zoning board before a building permit can be issued.

We look at setbacks and lot coverage at the first visit, so you know early whether the addition you want needs a zoning step, which can add weeks or months to the schedule.

Historic districts and older neighborhoods

Parts of Salem, Marblehead and other North Shore towns are in local historic districts, where exterior changes visible from the street need approval from the historic district commission. Even outside those districts, an addition should respect the style of the house: rooflines, window proportions, siding and trim.

We plan for that review step when it applies and design details that look original to the house, which is also what protects resale value.

Near the water: conservation and wetlands

Coastal lots and properties near ponds, streams and marshes may fall under the Massachusetts Wetlands Protection Act, reviewed by the town conservation commission. Work within a buffer zone can require a filing and conditions for erosion control. It is common in towns along the water and around the many ponds of the North Shore, and it needs to be on the schedule from the start.

Engineering and plans

Every addition starts with a plan: a site plan showing setbacks, floor plans, elevations and a structural design for the foundation, framing and roof. An architect or designer usually handles the layout and look, and a structural engineer sizes beams, headers, footings and connections. Our About page states that all structural projects are pulled under licensed engineer review and town permits, and additions are no exception.

If you already have plans, we build from them and help catch anything that does not fit the existing house. If not, we can bring in the design help you need.

Foundations below the frost line

An addition needs a foundation that will not heave in a New England winter. In eastern Massachusetts footings are set below the frost line, typically around four feet deep. Depending on the design and the site, the addition may sit on a full basement, a crawlspace or a slab, and ledge, high groundwater or an old stone foundation next door can change the approach.

The foundation also has to meet the existing house cleanly, with proper drainage and waterproofing so the new space stays dry.

Tying the addition into the existing house

The joint between old and new is where additions fail if it is rushed. Floors need to meet at the same height, walls need to line up where the finishes will meet, and the roof and flashing at the connection have to shed water and snow for decades. Old houses are rarely level or square, so we lay out from the existing structure, not from a perfect drawing, and adjust the new framing to make the transition invisible.

Rooflines, siding and windows that match

The best compliment an addition can get is that nobody can tell it was added. That comes down to roof pitch and overhangs that relate to the main house, siding with the same exposure and profile, trim details carried through and windows that match in style and proportion. On older homes with clapboards or cedar shingles we match the existing materials as closely as possible, and our exterior painting crew ties the whole house together with one finish.

Heating, cooling and electrical capacity

New rooms need heat and cooling. Extending an old system is not always possible, so ductless mini-splits are a common choice for additions on the North Shore. The electrical service also has to carry the new circuits; older homes sometimes need a service upgrade. All of that work is done by licensed trades under their own permits, and we coordinate it inside the project schedule.

Insulation and the energy code

Additions are built to the current Massachusetts energy code, including the stretch energy code that many towns have adopted. That affects wall and roof assemblies, air sealing, windows and ventilation, and it is checked at inspection. A well-insulated addition is more comfortable than the old house next to it, and it helps prevent the ice dams that plague poorly insulated roofs in our winters.

Mudrooms and front entries

Small entry additions solve one of the most common complaints in New England homes: nowhere to put boots, coats and bags in winter. A mudroom addition with a bench, hooks and a durable floor, or a covered front entry that keeps snow off the steps, is a modest project with daily payoff. The covered porch and steps we built on Lowell Street in Wakefield, shown in the gallery, is a good example of a small exterior addition done right.

How long a home addition takes

Once construction starts, a typical addition takes about 8 to 16 weeks depending on size and complexity. Before that come design, engineering, zoning review if needed, and the building permit, which can take longer than the construction itself in some towns. We give you a written schedule and update it as inspections and deliveries are confirmed.

What drives the cost of a home addition

Additions are among the most variable projects in residential construction, which is why we do not publish a single price. The biggest cost drivers are size, foundation type, whether plumbing is involved (a bathroom or kitchen costs more than a family room), roof complexity, finish level and site conditions like ledge or access. Zoning, historic or conservation steps add design and permitting time.

What we do promise is a full, itemized written estimate after an on-site walkthrough, with labor, materials and permits broken out, so there are no guesses and no surprises.

First-floor additions for aging in place

More North Shore homeowners are planning to stay in their houses for decades, and a first-floor bedroom and bathroom is often the single change that makes that possible. Designing for it means wider doorways and halls, a curbless or low-threshold shower, blocking in the walls for future grab bars, good lighting and a step-free route from the driveway. These details cost little when they are planned from the start and a lot when they are retrofitted later.

Garage additions and rooms over the garage

A new garage bay or a room over an existing garage is a practical way to add space on many North Shore lots. Building over a garage usually means new roof framing, a stair and sometimes larger headers over the garage door opening, and the wall and ceiling between the garage and living space have specific fire separation requirements. Converting part of a garage into living space adds insulation, a raised or insulated floor and new framing where the door was.

Kitchen and dining additions

When a kitchen is too small to fix inside its own walls, adding onto it is often the answer. A rear addition can turn a cramped galley into an open kitchen with an island and a dining area, with the old exterior wall replaced by an engineered beam. Because we also handle kitchen remodeling, the addition and the new kitchen are planned together: plumbing locations, window placement, lighting and cabinet layout are set before the foundation is poured, not after.

Living at home during an addition

Most families stay in the house while we build. Much of an addition happens outside the existing walls, and the opening into the house is usually one of the last steps. We seal the work area, protect floors and paths, keep the site clean every day and tell you ahead of time when utilities will be interrupted or the house will be opened up.

What to ask any addition contractor

  • Are you HIC registered and insured, and can I see the certificates?
  • Who pulls the building permit, and who meets the inspectors?
  • Who is the engineer, and who handles zoning or historic review if needed?
  • Is the estimate itemized, with labor, materials and permits broken out?
  • How will you protect my house and family while the addition is connected?
  • What happens after the job is done?

Our answer to the last one: a final walkthrough together, and a check-in one year later, because if something needs attention we want to be the ones who fix it.

Home Additions FAQ

Who builds home additions on the North Shore, MA?

Premium Pro Contractors is a licensed, insured general contractor based in Peabody that builds home additions across the North Shore, MA, handling engineering coordination, permits, foundation, framing, trades and finishes under one contract.

How long does a home addition take on the North Shore?

Construction typically takes about 8 to 16 weeks depending on size. Design, engineering, zoning review and permits come before that and vary by town.

Do I need a permit for a home addition in Massachusetts?

Yes. Additions need a building permit, with plumbing and electrical permits pulled by licensed trades, and may also need zoning, historic district or conservation approvals depending on the lot. We coordinate the process.

How much does a home addition cost?

It depends on size, foundation, plumbing, roof complexity, finishes and site conditions. We provide a full, itemized written estimate after an on-site walkthrough instead of a guess.

Is it cheaper to build up or build out?

It depends on the house. Building out needs a new foundation and must fit setbacks and lot coverage; building up needs the existing structure to carry a new floor, confirmed by an engineer. We review both at the walkthrough.

Can I live in my house during an addition?

Most families do. Much of the work happens outside the existing walls, and we seal off the work area, protect floors and clean up daily.
