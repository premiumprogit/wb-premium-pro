Prep Is Where the Job Is Won or Lost

Anyone can roll paint on a wall. What separates a lasting interior job from a disappointing one is the prep underneath it. On older Reading homes with plaster walls, that means patching cracks, skimming rough areas smooth, sanding glossy old trim so new paint bonds, and caulking the gaps where trim meets wall. Skip that and the new color will highlight every flaw instead of hiding it, and the trim paint will chip where it was never sanded. We take the slower, more careful road on prep because it is the only way a finish reads clean and holds up. It is also why our estimate is a real assessment of your home rather than a guess.

Plaster Walls in Older Reading Homes

Many Reading homes built before the 1950s still have their original plaster walls and ceilings. Plaster is solid and quiet, and it takes paint beautifully when it is prepared correctly. It also cracks, bulges where the keys behind it have let go, and shows every old repair under a fresh coat of paint if the prep is rushed.

Because we are a general contractor and not only a painting crew, plaster repair is part of the same job. We open and fill cracks so they do not telegraph back through the new finish, re-secure loose areas, skim coat sections that are too rough to paint, and prime repairs so the final coats dry to one even sheen. In homes that mix plaster with newer drywall from past renovations, we pay attention to the transition between the two, where most visible lines show up. The result is a wall that looks like it was always smooth, not a fresh color over old damage.

Lead-Safe Interior Painting for Homes Built Before 1978

If your Reading home was built before 1978, there is a real chance that some of the older layers of paint contain lead, especially on windows, doors, trim and stair parts. Lead paint is not a danger when it is intact and covered, but sanding, scraping or cutting it creates dust that is harmful, particularly to young children and pregnant women.

Federal rules from the EPA require contractors who disturb painted surfaces in pre-1978 homes to follow lead-safe work practices, and Massachusetts has its own lead law focused on homes where young children live. In practice, that means containing the work area, using methods that keep dust down, cleaning carefully at the end of each day and verifying the cleanup. When we walk through your home for the estimate, we ask about the age of the house and plan the prep around it, so older trim and windows get repainted safely instead of being sanded in the open. If you are not sure when your home was built, the town assessor records usually show it.

Ceilings: Water Stains, Cracks and Popcorn Texture

Ceilings are the surface homeowners notice last and the one that most changes how a room feels. A yellowed or patched ceiling makes fresh walls look tired. Painting a ceiling well takes more care than most people expect, because it is overhead, it shows lap marks in raking light and it often hides old problems.

Water stains from an old leak need a stain-blocking primer before any finish coat, or they bleed right back through new paint within weeks. Hairline cracks in plaster ceilings need to be opened, filled and primed. Popcorn or textured ceilings can be painted, repaired or, in some homes, removed and skim coated for a smooth finish, which is a bigger job that we price separately and explain in the estimate. We always confirm the source of any stain has been fixed before we cover it. Painting over an active leak only hides a problem that will come back.

Bathrooms, Laundry Rooms and Other Damp Spaces

Bathrooms and laundry rooms are the hardest rooms on paint. Steam, splashes and poor ventilation lead to peeling, mildew spots and soft drywall around tubs and showers. A fresh coat of standard wall paint will not last long in those conditions.

In damp rooms we clean and treat any mildew before painting, repair damaged drywall, prime properly and use a moisture and mildew resistant paint in a finish that wipes clean. We also look at the cause: if the exhaust fan is weak or vents into the attic, or if caulk around the tub has failed, we point it out so the new paint is not fighting the same problem. Small details matter here, such as painting behind the toilet tank and inside the vanity opening, because those are the spots where moisture damage starts.

Picking a Sheen: Flat, Matte, Eggshell, Satin and Semi-Gloss

The sheen of a paint is how much light it reflects, and it changes both how a room looks and how well the walls hold up. Choosing the wrong sheen is one of the most common reasons a new paint job disappoints.

  • Flat and matte: hide wall imperfections best and look soft and rich. Great for ceilings and low-traffic rooms such as formal living rooms and adult bedrooms.
  • Eggshell: a slight glow with better washability. A reliable choice for most living spaces, hallways and bedrooms.
  • Satin: more durable and easier to clean. Good for kitchens, kids' rooms, mudrooms and high-traffic halls.
  • Semi-gloss: hard and scrubbable. Our standard for trim, doors, cabinets and bathrooms.

Older plaster walls with some texture usually look best in a lower sheen, because shine highlights every ripple. We walk you through this during the estimate so you are not guessing at the paint store.

How Long a Good Interior Paint Job Lasts

How long interior paint looks good depends far more on preparation and product than on the color. A job done with proper repair, primer and quality paint will hold its color and wash clean for years, while a quick coat over dirty or damaged walls can start to show problems within months.

High-traffic areas such as hallways, stairs, mudrooms and kids' rooms wear faster than a guest room or formal living room, and trim takes more abuse than walls. That is why we choose more durable finishes for those spaces. We also leave you the leftover paint, labeled by room, so small touch-ups later blend in instead of standing out as a different shade.

Wallpaper Removal and Surface Prep

Older homes often hide one or more layers of wallpaper, sometimes painted over by a previous owner. Painting directly over wallpaper rarely ends well: seams show through, edges lift and moisture from the new paint can make the paper bubble. The right way is to remove it, but the method depends on what is underneath.

On plaster, wallpaper usually comes off cleanly with scoring, steam or a remover, followed by washing off the old paste. On drywall that was never primed, the paper face of the drywall can tear, so we work more carefully and repair any damage with a skim coat. Once the paper and paste are gone, we prime with a primer designed for that surface so the finish coats go on evenly. Wallpaper removal is priced separately in your estimate, because the effort varies a lot from one room to the next.

Drywall Patching, Nail Pops and Small Repairs

Almost every interior painting job includes some wall repair. Nail pops, dents from furniture, holes from old shelves and TV mounts, cracked corner bead and gaps where walls meet ceilings are all common, especially in homes that have settled over time.

Small repairs are included in our standard prep: we reset popped nails or screws, fill holes and dents, sand them smooth and spot prime so the repair does not flash through the new paint. Larger damage, such as water-damaged drywall or a section that needs to be replaced, is quoted clearly before we start. Because our crew handles drywall and carpentry as part of the same job, you do not have to bring in a separate handyman before the painters can begin.
